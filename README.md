The Deadline Desk: How a Legal VA Protects Your Calendar
Treat docketing as a repeatable control process, not a task completed only when someone remembers it.

Build the matter calendar from source documents
Direct the VA to review court orders, scheduling orders, notices, service records, and attorney-approved matter instructions. Require the VA to identify every visible event, deadline, cutoff, hearing, conference, filing date, and follow-up milestone.

Have the VA record the source document, date received, matter number, deadline type, and person responsible. Require attorney review for any deadline that involves legal interpretation or judgment.

Separate external and internal deadlines
Record the court or agency deadline as the external deadline. Add internal deadlines earlier than the official date for drafting, review, client approval, filing preparation, and quality control.

For example, a filing due on Friday may require an internal attorney review by Tuesday and a document-completion checkpoint by Monday. This structure creates working time without changing the official deadline.

Use trigger-based entries
Build calendar rules around events such as:

Date of service
Date an order is entered
Discovery cutoff
Deposition date
Mediation date
Trial date
Client document receipt
Expert report delivery

Use the trigger to start a task sequence. Do not ask the VA to interpret a statute, choose a legal position, or calculate a deadline that requires attorney judgment. Escalate uncertain entries immediately.

Add layered reminders
Set reminders at 30, 14, 7, and 3 days when the matter and court rules justify those intervals. Adjust the cadence for urgent filings, short response periods, hearings, and high-risk matters.

Ask the VA to send a short daily or weekly exception report showing overdue confirmations, upcoming deadlines, unassigned tasks, and entries requiring attorney review.

Confirm and review
Require the VA to confirm each critical entry back to the responsible attorney or paralegal. Conduct a weekly docket review that checks new matters, recent orders, upcoming deadlines, completed tasks, and unresolved escalations.

This process reduces dependence on memory. It does not guarantee that every error will be prevented. It gives your firm a clearer control point and a documented review rhythm.

Where Billable Time Actually Leaks
Administrative work also affects the money your firm has already earned.

Industry reports often state that small-firm professionals lose more than five hours per week to non-billable administrative work [VNS to confirm]. Realization suffers when attorneys submit time entries late, use vague narratives, omit small billable tasks, or reconstruct several days at once.

Unbilled work in progress can age quietly. A client may receive an incomplete invoice. A write-off decision may occur before anyone identifies the missing detail. A collection reminder may be delayed because no one owns the follow-up queue.

Use a VA to create consistency without allowing the VA to make billing decisions.

The Realization Recovery Work a VA Can Own
Assign the VA to maintain the process around billing while the attorney approves every time entry, invoice, adjustment, write-off, and collection decision.

A legal VA can:

Send daily time-entry prompts based on approved schedules and matter activity.

Flag entries that may be vague, incomplete, late, or at risk of write-off.

Maintain a client billing follow-up cadence.

What a Legal VA Never Does
Protect the firm with written boundaries. A legal VA should never:

Provide legal advice or explain legal strategy.
Choose a legal position or recommend a litigation response.
Sign pleadings, agreements, waivers, or client communications requiring attorney authority.
Approve settlements or payment arrangements.
Execute fee agreements or conflict waivers.
Make privilege determinations.
Approve invoices, write-offs, refunds, or collection actions.
Assume the attorney’s review or supervision responsibility.

Give the VA task-level authority, not legal authority. Use individual credentials, role-based permissions, secure file access, and audit trails. Review critical outputs before filing, billing, or sending them externally. The American Bar Association provides a useful starting point for professional responsibility resources.

How Fast Can You Go Live?
Use a focused 7–10 day plan:

Days 1–2: Choose one queue, map the docket or billing workflow, define escalation rules, and provide approved access.
Days 3–5: Train the VA on firm procedures, court-specific deadlines, software, naming standards, and sample matters.
Days 6–8: Pilot the workflow on docket review, time-entry follow-up, or pre-bill preparation.
Days 9–10: Review quality, correct gaps, finalize the SOP, and expand only after attorney approval.

Start with one practice group or one queue. Measure before adding complexity.
